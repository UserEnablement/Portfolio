Learning Enablement & Ops · Leadership Development

Designing a remote onboarding framework around what new hires actually need to feel.

A full-lifecycle onboarding system — pre-boarding through 90-day review — built for a fully remote organization and structured around a five-need psychological model, not just a task checklist.

Scope: Full onboarding lifecycle Format: Framework, checklists, surveys, dashboard design Context: Remote-first organization
The problem

Onboarding was fragmented across ad hoc emails, no shared "source of truth," and no way to tell whether a new hire's early experience actually predicted whether they'd stay. In a fully remote environment, the informal cues that normally build belonging — hallway chats, watching how a team works — don't exist by default; they have to be designed in.

The approach

Rather than building a generic task checklist, I mapped every onboarding touchpoint to the specific psychological need it was meant to satisfy — Clarity, Support, Belonging, Significance, and Purpose — then designed the practices, roles, and measurement system around closing gaps in that model.

The output

A three-phase framework (pre-boarding → first week → first 90 days), role-specific checklists for four stakeholder groups, a Buddy/Guide peer-support system, two longitudinal pulse surveys, and a dashboard design turning survey data into a departmental early-warning system.

Design rationale

Every onboarding practice earns its place by meeting a specific need

Instead of asking "what tasks does a new hire need to complete," I asked "what does a new hire need to feel — and by when — for onboarding to actually work?" I used a five-need model as the backbone, and audited every existing and proposed practice against it. Anything that didn't map to a need got cut or reworked.

Distributed ownership

Four stakeholder checklists, one shared outcome

A framework only works if it's actually executed — so I broke it into role-specific checklists for the four groups with a stake in a new hire's first 90 days, each scoped to what only they can own.

Peer support system

Buddy vs. Guide — two deliberately different roles

Early drafts conflated "friendly peer" with "tactical resource" into a single role, which overloaded whoever took it on. I split it in two: a rotating Buddy for low-stakes social connection, and a tenured Guide who owns cultural navigation, tool proficiency, and — critically — the knowledge-management risk of being a single point of failure.

Measurement

Two pulse surveys, mapped to the same driver model

A 30-day survey and a 6-month survey use different wording — because what matters at day 30 (was I set up to succeed?) isn't what matters at month 6 (do I see a future here?) — but every question maps back to the same five drivers. That shared backbone is what makes the two surveys comparable over time: a driver that scores low at 30 days can be tracked forward to see whether it recovered, stayed flat, or kept sliding by month 6.

Turning survey data into action

Dashboard design: from "how do people feel" to "where do we intervene"

I designed the analytics layer, not just requested it. Two ideas drive the layout: a Sentiment Gap — the 6-month score minus the 30-day score, per driver — which catches the "honeymoon dip" where early excitement fades faster than it should; and a ranked Fix-It List that forces leadership to act on the driver losing the most ground first, instead of reacting to whichever complaint was loudest.

What this demonstrates

Systems thinking, not just content authoring

This piece is deliberately different from a course or a job aid: it's evidence of end-to-end program design — needs analysis, stakeholder-specific delivery, a defensible measurement model, and an analytics layer that turns survey data into a prioritized action list for leadership. It's the same underlying skill set used to build the leadership development curriculum and change-management program elsewhere in this portfolio, applied to the first 90 days of the employee lifecycle instead of a training rollout.

Organizational branding and any identifying details have been generalized throughout; the framework, driver model, and dashboard logic are shown exactly as designed.
